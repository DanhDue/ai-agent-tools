# Rubric — `high-everywhere`

Grade each reply independently, PASS or FAIL per criterion, quoting the reply.

Standard: Dan Olsen, *The Lean Product Playbook* (Wiley, 2015), chapter 5.

---

### C1 — Did the reply challenge being best at everything?

**PASS** if the reply treats all-High as a problem to resolve before proceeding. **FAIL** if it
accepts the grid, or only compliments the ambition.

---

### C2 — Did the reply require exactly one designated winner?

Not "focus more" or "prioritise" — a single named benefit the product will be best at.

**PASS** if the reply asks the founder to pick one of the three. **FAIL** if it offers only general
advice about focus, or accepts two.

---

### C3 — Did the reply require a deliberate Medium or Low?

The scored-down cell is the only direct evidence in the grid that a decision was made, and it is what
funds the win. In Olsen's own completed example the author's product is Medium on one performance
benefit and **Low** on another.

**PASS** if the reply asks which benefit the founder is willing to be Medium or Low at. **FAIL** if
no trade-off is requested.

---

### C4 — Did the reply state the parity requirement?

Google won by being best at the benefit that mattered most **and having comparable or better
performance on the other dimensions**. Both halves are required: winning one axis while falling
behind on another means the win is never evaluated.

**PASS** if the reply says the non-winning benefits must still hold parity. **FAIL** if it implies the
others can simply be deprioritised, or does not address them.

---

### C5 — Did the reply distinguish planned scores from measured ones?

The founder is designing, not assessing a shipped product. Every cell in their column is a
hypothesis, and a planned High is a commitment to spend.

**PASS** if the reply marks the scores as projections to be tested, or asks for measurable numbers
where the benefit allows ("time to send first invoice" is measurable in seconds). **FAIL** if it
treats the scores as facts.

---

### C6 — Did the reply engage with the no-legacy argument rather than ignore it?

"Fresh codebase, no accumulated compromise" is a real advantage. A reply that ignores it is not
answering the argument made.

**PASS** if the reply addresses it — accepting it as genuine while noting it does not survive contact
with finite engineering capacity, or that competitors' breadth is itself the product of years of
spend. **FAIL** if it goes unmentioned.

---
